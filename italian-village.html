Italian Village: a Columbus renting and living guide

What landlords and tenants should know about Italian Village—how it grew just north of downtown, its Italianate and Queen Anne homes, doubles, and rowhouses, the Italian Village Commission’s exterior review, Short North permit parking, COTA service on High and Fourth Streets, and ZIP-level voucher figures—without inventing market rents.

Last updated October 22, 2026. Neighborhood facts below come from the City of Columbus, the Italian Village Commission’s guidelines, local history sources, the Short North Alliance, Columbus Recreation and Parks, COTA stop data, and CMHA tables cited in Sources. Asking-rent medians are not invented here; see Rent Pulse and Fair Market Rents for verified figures.

Where Italian Village sits

Italian Village is a neighborhood inside the City of Columbus, directly north of downtown and on the east side of the Short North. It is commonly described as bounded by Interstate 670 on the south, East Fifth Avenue on the north, North High Street on the west, and the railroad tracks on the east. Because it is part of Columbus itself, Columbus city services, the Columbus income tax, and Columbus City Code rules for landlords and tenants apply.

Inside those edges the streets run on a grid with alleys behind most lots. North Fourth Street, East Fifth Avenue, and East Second Avenue are the main corridors, and the City’s 2000 Italian Village East Redevelopment Plan describes the eastern half of the neighborhood as running from the Fourth Street corridor east to the tracks. The High Street frontage is shared with the Short North Arts District, so a “Short North” listing can sit inside Italian Village. For the commercial strip itself, see our Short North guide; for other neighborhood spotlights, start at the Neighborhoods hub or compare with Victorian Village across High Street, German Village, Olde Towne East, Clintonville, and Dublin.

How Italian Village got here

The Italian Village Commission’s guidelines describe the area as one of Columbus’s first suburbs. The land opened up after the Columbus–Worthington Pike, today’s High Street, was built in 1823, and the area was annexed to Columbus in 1862. Streetcar service, including the North Columbus Street Railway Company, helped it fill in, and by 1899 the street system included the long alleys that once served horse stables behind the houses.

Several groups lived here before and alongside the Italian families the neighborhood is named for. The guidelines note Irish residents in the southern part of the area around 1850–1870 and a small African American community in the northeast section that founded Bethany Baptist Church in 1882. In the 1890s Italian immigrants moved in from nearby Flytown and worked in quarries, construction trades, railroads, and High Street businesses. St. John the Baptist Italian Catholic Church was founded in 1896 and became the center of the Italian Catholic community; the parish’s own history and a centennial history held by the Columbus Metropolitan Library document its early years. Factories such as the Jeffrey Manufacturing Company, which made coal-mining equipment, and the Berry Brothers Bolt Works were within walking distance.

After World War II the neighborhood declined. Many original residents left for the suburbs, large houses were cut up into apartments, and in the early 1950s parts of several streets were cleared for the inner belt highway and urban renewal. Residents and property owners responded by forming the Italian Village Society in 1973, and the City created the Italian Village Commission and historic district the same year. By the early 1980s the guidelines describe middle-income households returning, and rehabilitation has continued since.

Housing stock: what you will actually rent

According to the Commission’s guidelines, about 80 percent of the buildings are residential, mostly single-family homes, doubles, and rowhouses, with commercial uses concentrated on Fourth Street and Fifth Avenue and industrial buildings scattered through the area, especially east of Sixth Street and north of Second Avenue. Homes sit on narrow lots close to the street with alleys in the rear. The guidelines describe them as mostly Italianate and Queen Anne in style, typically two to two-and-a-half stories, with brick rowhouses under flat roofs and Italianate cornices, and many American Foursquare houses with broad front porches. Some former industrial buildings, including the Berry Brothers Bolt Works, have been renovated for new uses.

For landlords, that mix means a large share of rentals are units in older converted houses and doubles, where separate utility meters, older wiring and plumbing, porch and stair maintenance, and alley trash pickup are worth confirming before you list. For tenants, ask whether a unit is one half of a double, a floor of a converted house, or a newer building, because noise, storage, laundry, and parking can differ a lot between those three on the same block.

The Italian Village Commission and exterior changes

The City of Columbus says the Italian Village Commission and the Italian Village Historic District were established by Ordinance 1579-73 in 1973, and the Commission’s guidelines were adopted in 1990. The Commission has seven members appointed by the Mayor who serve without pay. It reviews applications and issues Certificates of Appropriateness for exterior alterations to properties inside the district under Columbus City Code chapters 3116 and 3119 and the Italian Village guidelines. Projects on the High Street corridor in Italian Village and Victorian Village are reviewed under the Short North Design Guidelines instead.

The guidelines cover a long list of exterior items, including fences, sidewalks and parking areas, roofs, gutters, siding, masonry, porches, doors, windows, storm windows, garages, exterior paint, awnings, signs, storefronts, and new construction. The City publishes a staff approval list for work staff can sign off on without a full hearing. Other applications go to the Commission, which generally meets on the second Tuesday of the month at 4 p.m. at 111 N. Front St., room 204. Its 2026 calendar lists November 10 (application deadline October 14) and December 8 (deadline November 11). Applications are now filed through the City’s Citizens Access Portal rather than by email, and staff can be reached at IVC@columbus.gov.

In practice, a landlord planning new windows, a replacement porch, a fence, or a parking pad should check the guidelines and get a Certificate of Appropriateness before ordering materials or booking a contractor, and should build the hearing calendar into turnover timelines. Tenants should know that exterior changes such as satellite dishes, signs, or fences usually need the owner’s involvement and may need Commission approval.

Parking: part of the Short North plan

Italian Village is inside the Short North parking program, and the City’s plan for it lists the Italian Village Commission and the Italian Village Society among the groups represented on its parking benefit district. According to the Short North Alliance, eligible addresses can apply online for annual resident permits at $25 each, one per licensed driver and up to two per address, and annual guest permits are also $25. Free-to-request 24-hour guest passes let a resident or guest pay $3 to park in a subzone of the resident’s permit zone, up to 300 passes a year. Resident permits do not exempt drivers from paying in general pay-to-park areas such as High Street and the blocks directly next to it.

The City’s permit portal FAQ notes that some addresses on permitted streets are not eligible for permits. Before signing a lease, check the address on the City’s permit eligibility map, find out whether the unit has an off-street or alley space, and read the posted signs on the block. Landlords should state in the listing whether parking is included rather than assuming a permit will be available.

Getting around: COTA on High and Fourth

COTA Line 2 runs on North High Street along the neighborhood’s western edge. Inside Italian Village, COTA Lines 4 (Indianola/Lockbourne) and 12 (McKinley/Fields) stop on North Fourth Street, including at Lincoln Street and East Second Avenue, and Line 4 continues north on Fourth Street toward the University District and Clintonville. Eligible Short North workers and residents may also qualify for the Short North C-pass for free COTA rides; see our Short North guide for details. Check COTA’s current timetables for frequency on the hours and days you actually travel.

Italian Village Park and nearby landmarks

Columbus Recreation and Parks lists Italian Village Park at 39 E. Hubbard Ave. as a 0.835-acre neighborhood park, open 7 a.m. to 11 p.m. St. John the Baptist Italian Catholic Church remains a neighborhood landmark. Goodale Park, the North Market, and the Greater Columbus Convention Center are a short walk west and south across High Street, and downtown jobs are just south of I-670.

Vouchers, ZIPs, and what the SAFMR tables say

Italian Village spans ZIP codes 43201 and 43215, and the two carry very different voucher figures. CMHA’s FY2027 Small Area Fair Market Rents, effective October 1, 2026, at 100% of SAFMR, list $1,420 for a one-bedroom and $1,690 for a two-bedroom in 43201, and $1,970 for a one-bedroom and $2,350 for a two-bedroom in 43215. These are payment-standard benchmarks, not asking rents and not automatic rent approvals; contract rent still goes through rent-reasonableness review, and utility allowances affect the gross-rent comparison. Our Fair Market Rents explainer covers how they work.

Inside Columbus city limits, Columbus City Code 4551.03 restricts refusing to rent based on lawful source of income, including vouchers. That rule and Ohio’s deposit, notice, and repair rules are covered in our Ohio and Columbus landlord-tenant law guide.

What landlords and tenants should check before signing

  • Exterior work. Confirm whether planned repairs need a Certificate of Appropriateness and how long that will take.
  • Unit type. Know whether it is half of a double, part of a converted house, a rowhouse, or a newer building, and what is shared.
  • Parking. Check permit eligibility for the exact address, off-street or alley space, and guest options.
  • Utilities. Ask which utilities are separately metered and who pays for water, trash, and common-area lighting.
  • Noise and location. Units near High Street and Fourth Street see more traffic and nightlife than interior blocks.
  • ZIP for vouchers. Pull the ZIP from the address, not the neighborhood name, before doing voucher math.

Is Italian Village a historic district?

Yes. The City of Columbus says the Italian Village Commission and the Italian Village Historic District were established by Ordinance 1579-73 in 1973, and the Commission’s guidelines were adopted in 1990. The Commission has seven mayoral appointees and issues Certificates of Appropriateness for exterior alterations inside the district under Columbus City Code chapters 3116 and 3119.

Do I need approval to change the outside of a rental in Italian Village?

Usually, yes. Exterior alterations at properties inside the Italian Village Historic District need a Certificate of Appropriateness. Some items can be approved by staff, and others go to the Commission’s monthly hearing, generally on the second Tuesday at 4 p.m. at 111 N. Front St. Applications are filed through the City’s Citizens Access Portal, and the High Street frontage is reviewed under the Short North Design Guidelines.

How does parking work in Italian Village?

Italian Village is part of the Short North parking plan. Eligible addresses can get annual resident permits for $25 each, one per licensed driver and up to two per address, and annual guest permits are also $25. Free-to-request 24-hour guest passes let a guest pay $3 to park in a subzone of your permit zone. Resident permits do not exempt you from paying in general pay-to-park areas such as High Street, and some addresses are not eligible for permits at all.

Lines 4 (Indianola/Lockbourne) and 12 (McKinley/Fields) stop on North Fourth Street inside Italian Village, including at Lincoln Street and East Second Avenue.
