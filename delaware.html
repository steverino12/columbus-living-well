Delaware: a renting and living guide to the Delaware County seat north of Columbus

What landlords and tenants should know about the City of Delaware, the fast-growing county seat on the Olentangy River, including its 1.85% city income tax and its unusual half credit for tax paid where you work, the zero school district income tax in Delaware City Schools, the absence of a city rental registry, the annual short-term rental registration, the city and county lodging taxes, the single city bill for water, sewer, stormwater, and trash, Delaware County Transit’s FLEX service in place of COTA, and HUD voucher figures for ZIP 43015, without inventing market rents.

Last updated November 12, 2026. Facts below come from the City of Delaware, its adopted ordinances, the Ohio Department of Taxation, the Ohio Revised Code, Delaware County, Delaware County Transit, COTA, NCES, HUD, AHACO, the Delaware Gazette, a Rental Rule Book summary of the city’s short-term rental chapter, and Wikipedia’s summary of the city, all cited in Sources. Asking-rent medians are not invented here; see Rent Pulse and Fair Market Rents for verified figures.

A county seat city with its own rules

Delaware is the county seat of Delaware County, a separate city north of Columbus along U.S. Route 23 with the Olentangy River running through it. Wikipedia’s summary says it was founded in 1808 and incorporated in 1816, and the city’s own history page credits Moses Byxbe, who arrived from Berkshire County, Massachusetts, in 1804, with filing the “plan of the town of Delaware” on May 9, 1808. Rutherford B. Hayes, the 19th president, was born in Delaware, and Ohio Wesleyan University, chartered in 1842, still anchors the downtown along South Sandusky Street.

The 2020 census counted 41,302 residents, 15,759 households, and 16,863 housing units on about 18.95 square miles of land, according to Wikipedia’s summary of the census. Of those housing units, 6.5% were vacant; the homeowner vacancy rate was 1.2% and the rental vacancy rate was 9.7%. About 35.8% of households included children under 18, and 50.8% were married couples. The city says its population reached 48,628 in 2025, that it has almost doubled since 2000, and that the median age is 34.5. It also reports a median household income of $74,130 from the 2020 Census.

Delaware is more of a freestanding small city than a bedroom suburb. The city’s list of top employers is led by Kroger’s grocery and distribution operations (about 2,496 employees), Ohio Wesleyan University (1,571), Delaware County government (1,114), OhioHealth (1,022), and Delaware City Schools (996), followed by Advance Stores, Willow Brook, the city itself, PPG Industries, and JEGS. The city says the downtown is 94% occupied, with about 310,000 square feet of ground-floor commercial space, roughly 60% of it retail, restaurant, and entertainment.

Because Delaware is a separate city, Columbus-only rules such as the Columbus rental registry and Renter’s Choice do not apply. Not every Delaware, Ohio mailing address (ZIP 43015) is inside the city, either. The city’s planning FAQ tells people who do not live in the city to contact the Delaware County Building Department (740-833-2200) and their township zoning inspector, and the city also administers a separate income tax for the Berkshire Township joint economic development district. Confirm that a parcel is inside the city limits before relying on anything below.

Income tax: 1.85% with only a half credit for work-city tax

Under Chapter 192 of its codified ordinances, the City of Delaware levies a 1.85% income tax on residents and resident businesses, and on nonresidents and nonresident businesses earning income in the city. Unlike Powell and many other suburbs that use the Regional Income Tax Agency (RITA), Delaware collects its own tax: its own Income Tax Department at City Hall, 1 S. Sandusky Street, handles filing and payment (740-203-1225).

The detail that matters most is the credit. Delaware gives residents only a partial credit for tax paid to the city where they work: the lesser of 50% of the tax paid to the other city, or 0.925% of the income taxed by both cities. In practice, most residents who commute to a taxing city still owe Delaware something. Using the city’s formula, a resident earning $60,000 in wages in Columbus pays Columbus $1,500 at 2.5%. Delaware’s full tax would be $1,110; the credit is the lesser of $750 (half the Columbus tax) or $555 (0.925% of $60,000), so the resident owes Delaware $555 on top of the Columbus withholding. Someone who works in a township with no municipal income tax gets no credit and owes the full 1.85%.

The rate and credit have been on the ballot. Delaware voters rejected a five-year increase to 2.2% in March 2024, and in November 2024 they rejected Issue 3, which would have raised the rate to 2.30% and the work-city credit to 65%, by 63.85% to 36.15%, according to the Delaware Gazette. City leaders had said they might eliminate the credit if Issue 3 failed; a Citizen Portal summary of the February 24, 2025 council meeting reports that council voted 4–3 against Ordinance 24-100, which would have repealed it, and the city’s current FAQ still describes the half credit. Check the city’s FAQ each year in case council revisits it.

Delaware has mandatory filing: every resident must file a return by April 15, even if no tax is due. Since tax year 2024, income of residents under 18 is exempt, and residents 65 or older with only non-taxable retirement income may be exempted from future filing. Taxpayers who expect to owe $200 or more beyond withholding and credits must make quarterly estimated payments. The city charges a 15% penalty on late tax and 9% annual interest for 2026. One point for landlords: the city’s FAQ says losses from rental property, self-employment, and partnerships may not be used to offset W-2 wages, so a rental loss will not reduce the tax on a paycheck.

School district income tax: none in Delaware City Schools

The city says three public school districts reach inside its limits: Delaware City Schools, Olentangy Local Schools, and Buckeye Valley Local Schools. The Ohio Department of Taxation’s school district income tax table lists Delaware City School District (district 2103) at 0.00% and Olentangy LSD (2104) at 0.00%, but Buckeye Valley LSD (2102) at 1.00%. That 1% applies on top of the city tax for residents of the Buckeye Valley portion, so checking the school district is as important as checking the city line. Big Walnut LSD, elsewhere in the county, is listed at 0.75%.

NCES lists Delaware City (district ID 3904387) as a large suburban district serving grades PK–12 in 7 schools, with 5,650 students and about 290 classroom teachers (FTE) in the 2024–2025 school year, a student–teacher ratio of 19.48. Rutherford B. Hayes High School and John C. Dempsey Middle School are its secondary schools. For comparison, the city puts Olentangy at more than 24,000 students and Buckeye Valley at about 2,300.

Rental registration: county auditor, plus city code enforcement

We did not find a City of Delaware rental registration, rental license, or proactive rental inspection program for long-term rentals. Ohio Revised Code 5323.02 still requires owners of residential rental property to register with the county auditor, which for every Delaware parcel is the Delaware County Auditor.

Rental housing is still covered by the city’s property maintenance rules. Chapter 1303 of the codified ordinances adopts the International Property Maintenance Code, with the Planning and Community Development Department as the enforcing agency. The city’s Code Enforcement Division, which it describes as two officers who patrol and investigate complaints, mails formal notices of violation to owners by both certified and regular mail, and residents can report problems through the city’s Del-AWARE app. In May 2026 council held a hearing and second reading on Ordinance 26-23, which amends the property maintenance code and adds a separate Exterior Property Maintenance Code (Chapter 1311) for siding, paint, yards, and other outside conditions; owners should check the city’s code for the final adopted text.

The city’s utility department also treats rentals differently in one respect: it requires a $100 deposit on utility accounts for residential rental properties only, covered in the utilities section below.

Fair housing: state and federal law, no local voucher protection found

Delaware is not on AHACO’s list of ten central Ohio communities that have adopted source-of-income protections: Bexley, Columbus, Gahanna, Grandview Heights, Pickerington, Reynoldsburg, Upper Arlington, Westerville, Whitehall, and Worthington. We did not find a local ordinance requiring landlords to accept housing vouchers.

The city’s Planning and Community Development department runs a Fair Housing and Grants page with brochures on fair housing basics, advertising guidelines, Ohio tenant-landlord law, and the Ohio Civil Rights Commission’s rights and responsibilities guide, and it names the Planning and Zoning Administrator, (740) 203-1600 option 2, as the contact. Ohio Revised Code Chapter 4112 and the federal Fair Housing Act bar housing discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, ancestry, disability, familial status, and, under Ohio law, military status. Without a local source-of-income rule, a Delaware landlord is not required by city law to accept a voucher, but every applicant should be screened against the same written standards.

Short-term rentals: an annual city registration

Delaware has a dedicated short-term rental chapter. Chapter 1133 of the Planning and Zoning Code was added when council replaced the zoning code with Ordinance 25-38 on July 14, 2025, and Ordinance 26-06, passed February 23, 2026, amended the registration section. The city’s code is hosted on Municode, which we could not open directly, so the points below follow Rental Rule Book’s section-by-section summary of Chapter 1133, read from Municode in October 2026:

  • Short-term rentals are allowed by right in the C-DC (downtown) district and on lots used for one- or two-family dwellings, but each needs an annual registration from the Planning and Community Development Department.
  • After an application is complete, Code Enforcement and the Fire Department inspect the property; fees set by council are paid after the inspection is passed. We did not find the fee amount.
  • Registrations run from issuance through December 31 of the same year, and operators must reapply every year (Section 1133.04(b)).
  • Only one registration is allowed per lot, and each person, household, or company may hold one hosted and one non-hosted registration. A hosted rental needs a permanent resident host on site; a non-hosted rental is a whole unit rented to one guest or group (Section 1133.03(c)).
  • No extra parking is required downtown; elsewhere, none is required if on-street parking is available within a block, otherwise one space per two possible separate guests (Section 1133.03(d)).
  • The registration number must appear in every listing, guests must receive the city’s Short-Term Rental Guidelines packet, and only one non-illuminated sign of up to two square feet is allowed.
  • A rental operating without registration must stop until it registers, and three code violations in one registration year mean revocation for the rest of that year (Section 1133.04(c)).

Because we worked from a summary rather than the code itself, confirm the current text, fee, and zoning district rules with the Planning and Community Development Department (740-203-1600) before listing a property.

Lodging tax: city and county

Delaware County levies a 6% lodging excise tax on rent paid by transient guests at establishments with five or more rooms for guests, except inside the City of Columbus, where the county rate is 3%. The county says bed and breakfasts with at least five guest rooms must collect it, operators file a monthly return due by the 23rd of the following month, and new lodging businesses must register within 30 days and post a Transient Occupancy Registration Certificate.

The City of Delaware has its own lodging tax as well: the Ohio Department of Taxation’s most recent lodging tax table we could open, Table S-4 for calendar year 2018, lists the City of Delaware at 3.00% alongside the county’s 6.00% (which the table notes includes a 3% Delaware County Agricultural Society tax). We did not find a more recent official rate table, so confirm the current city rate and whether it reaches a whole-home rental with fewer than five rooms with the city’s finance office before pricing a stay.

Leases and deposits: Ohio law applies

We did not find a local landlord-tenant code in Delaware, so Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5321 governs security deposits, repairs, entry notice, and eviction notices. Under that chapter, a landlord generally must return the deposit, with an itemized list of any deductions, within 30 days after the tenant gives up the unit and leaves a forwarding address. Ohio Revised Code 1901.02 gives the Delaware Municipal Court jurisdiction within Delaware County, so eviction cases for Delaware addresses are filed there rather than in Franklin County. Our Ohio landlord-tenant basics page walks through the statewide rules.

Utilities: one city bill for water, sewer, stormwater, and trash

Unlike Powell, where residents juggle several outside providers, the City of Delaware runs its own water and sewer systems and bills them together. Its utility billing office says a typical residential bill includes water, wastewater (sewer), and stormwater, plus curbside refuse and recycling. The city’s water plant draws primarily from the Olentangy River, can blend in groundwater from wells more than 200 feet deep, and treats an average of 3.65 million gallons a day for more than 12,000 customers through 190 miles of water lines. The sewer system has 175 miles of line and 12 pump stations and returns treated water to the Olentangy.

A few billing rules are worth putting in the lease:

  • Deposit. The city requires a $100 deposit on residential rental properties only. It can be waived for tenants with a “good payment record,” meaning no delinquency on a city account for two years, and it is generally credited back after two years of good payment or on the final bill.
  • Late payment. A bill not paid by the due date is assessed a 10% penalty, the city may shut off service by the 15th of the following month, and restoring service costs $50.
  • Starting service. Call 740-203-1250 x4 at least 24 hours before the start date. Since January 1, 2025, payments through the online billing portal carry a convenience fee, as do phone and credit or debit card payments.

We have not quoted per-gallon water and sewer charges here because we could not confirm the current rate schedule; water rates are set in Section 913.14 of the codified ordinances and sewer charges in Section 917.14, and the city’s fee schedule (Ordinance 24-91) adds a 50% surcharge to water rates for service outside the city. Ask Utility Billing for a recent bill history on the unit.

The city collects trash itself. The current fee is $27 a month, billed on the utility bill, and includes weekly trash, recycling, and yard waste collection, with yard waste picked up from mid-April through mid-December and in January. Households 65 and older get a 25% discount. An extra trash cart costs $15 a month and an extra recycling cart $10 a month, bag tags for overflow bags are $1.50 each, and bulk pickups are weekly by appointment. New residents can schedule a one-time free pickup of extra bagged trash during their first month, and departing residents get a free move-out pickup once they have scheduled their last day of service, which helps at turnover. Carts must be at the curb by 5 a.m. but not more than 24 hours early.

Getting around: Delaware County Transit FLEX, not COTA

Delaware does not appear on COTA’s current system map, and we found no COTA route serving the city. Local transit comes from Delaware County Transit (DCT), headquartered at 119 Henderson Court in the city.

DCT’s FLEX Delaware service offers curb-to-curb rides within Delaware City, booked in the app for the same day or up to two weeks ahead, Monday through Friday from 5 a.m. to 9 p.m. and Saturdays from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. The fare is $2.00 each way for ages 19 to 64, $1.00 for ages 5 to 18, riders 65 and up, and riders with disabilities, and free for children 4 and under with a paying adult. For trips elsewhere in the county, DCT’s Demand Response service runs weekdays from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., must be booked by noon the business day before, and costs $8 for trips up to 10 miles, $17 for 10 to 20 miles, and $29 for longer trips, with out-of-county trips limited to medical destinations.

Expansion is on hold. On November 3, 2026, Delaware County voters rejected a 0.2% sales and use tax for the new Delaware County Regional Transit Authority, 62,022 votes against out of 101,351 cast (61.2%), the Delaware Gazette reported. The tax would have paid for service expansions that included seven-day service and longer hours in Delaware and a commuter express service; DCT told the Gazette its board will use existing funding to maintain its current level of service. Most residents drive, using U.S. Routes 23, 36, and 42 and State Route 37, which pass through the city.

Parks and daily life

The city lists three larger community parks, a public swimming pool, miles of trails, an executive golf course, two free splash pads, and an urban forest of more than 18,000 trees, and the Delaware Community Center YMCA is another anchor. Ohio Wesleyan, which the city says has about 1,300 students on more than 200 acres next to downtown, adds a steady base of student and faculty renters near the center of town. The Delaware County Fairgrounds hosts the Little Brown Jug, a harness race in the pacers’ Triple Crown, during the county fair. Recycling note for tenants: the city collects old holiday string lights for recycling at Public Works, 440 E. William Street, from November 15, 2026 to January 18, 2027.

Those figures are well below the $1,830 one-bedroom and $2,180 two-bedroom benchmarks HUD lists for Powell’s ZIP 43065, a reminder that Delaware County is not one market. They are benchmarks that housing authorities use to set voucher payment standards, not asking rents, and ZIP 43015 includes township areas outside the city. The housing authority that administers a voucher sets the actual payment standard. For how these numbers are built, see our Fair Market Rents explainer.

What landlords and tenants should check before signing

  • Jurisdiction. Confirm the parcel is inside the City of Delaware rather than a surrounding township or the Berkshire Township JEDD; taxes, zoning, and utilities differ.
  • Income tax. Budget for Delaware’s 1.85% even if you work in Columbus; the half credit usually leaves a balance due at filing time, not withheld from paychecks.
  • School district. Delaware City Schools and Olentangy add no school income tax, but the Buckeye Valley portion adds 1%.
  • Registration. No city rental registry found; keep the Delaware County Auditor filing current under Ohio Revised Code 5323.02, and expect property maintenance code enforcement on complaint.
  • Short stays. Short-term rentals need an annual city registration, an inspection, and the registration number in every listing; one registration per lot.
  • Vouchers. No local source-of-income protection found; apply consistent written screening either way.
  • Utility account. One city bill covers water, sewer, stormwater, and $27 trash; plan for the $100 rental deposit and spell out who holds the account.
  • Transit. No COTA service; DCT FLEX covers trips within the city, and the failed November 2026 levy means no expansion soon.

Frequently asked questions

Is Delaware part of Columbus?

No. Delaware is a separate city and the county seat of Delaware County, north of Columbus along U.S. Route 23, with a 2020 census population of 41,302; the city says it reached 48,628 in 2025. It has its own council, income tax, and utilities, so Columbus-only rules such as Renter’s Choice and the Columbus rental registry do not apply. Not every Delaware mailing address (ZIP 43015) is inside the city, so confirm the parcel is within city limits.

What is the Delaware, Ohio income tax rate?

The City of Delaware’s income tax is 1.85%, collected by the city’s own Income Tax Department rather than RITA. Residents get only a partial credit for tax paid to the city where they work: the lesser of 50% of that tax or 0.925% of the income taxed by both cities. A resident earning $60,000 in Columbus would typically owe Delaware about $555 on top of Columbus withholding. All residents must file by April 15, though income of residents under 18 has been exempt since 2024.

Is there a school district income tax in Delaware?

Not in Delaware City Schools or Olentangy Local Schools, which the Ohio Department of Taxation lists at 0.00%. Buckeye Valley Local Schools, which also reaches inside the city limits, carries a 1.00% school district income tax. NCES reports Delaware City Schools had 5,650 students in 7 schools in 2024–2025.

Does Delaware have a rental registry?

We did not find a City of Delaware rental registration, rental license, or proactive rental inspection program for long-term rentals. Ohio Revised Code 5323.02 still requires owners to register residential rental property with the Delaware County Auditor, and the city enforces its property maintenance code through Code Enforcement.

Can I run an Airbnb in Delaware, Ohio?

Yes, with an annual city registration. Based on a published summary of Chapter 1133 of the city’s zoning code, short-term rentals are allowed by right downtown (C-DC) and on lots with one- or two-family dwellings, but need a Code Enforcement and Fire Department inspection, an annual registration that expires December 31, and the registration number in every listing. Only one registration is allowed per lot, and hosted rentals need a permanent resident host on site. Confirm current rules and fees with Planning and Community Development.

Is there a lodging tax in Delaware, Ohio?

Delaware County levies a 6% lodging tax on transient guests at establishments with five or more guest rooms outside Columbus, filed monthly with the county. The Ohio Department of Taxation’s 2018 lodging tax table also lists a 3.00% City of Delaware lodging tax. Confirm current rates, and whether they apply to a small whole-home rental, with the city and county before pricing a stay.

Can Delaware landlords refuse housing vouchers?

Delaware is not on AHACO’s list of ten central Ohio communities that have adopted source-of-income protections, and we found no local rule requiring landlords to accept vouchers. Ohio and federal fair housing laws still apply, covering race, color, religion, sex, national origin, ancestry, disability, familial status, and military status.

Who provides water, sewer, and trash in Delaware?

The City of Delaware does. One monthly city utility bill covers water, sewer, stormwater, and curbside trash, recycling, and yard waste; the trash fee is $27 a month, with a 25% discount for residents 65 and older. The city requires a $100 utility deposit for residential rental properties, which can be waived for customers with a two-year good payment record, and late bills carry a 10% penalty.

Does COTA serve Delaware, Ohio?

No. Delaware does not appear on COTA’s system map. Delaware County Transit’s FLEX service provides curb-to-curb rides within the city on weekdays from 5 a.m. to 9 p.m. and Saturdays from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. for $2.00 each way ($1.00 for youth, seniors, and riders with disabilities). Voters rejected a 0.2% county transit sales tax on November 3, 2026, so planned expansions are on hold.

What is the voucher payment standard in Delaware, Ohio?

For ZIP 43015, HUD’s FY2027 Small Area Fair Market Rents, effective October 1, 2026, are $1,260 for a studio, $1,350 for one bedroom, $1,610 for two, $1,920 for three, and $2,180 for four bedrooms. These are payment-standard benchmarks, not asking rents, and ZIP 43015 includes areas outside the city. The housing authority that administers a voucher sets the actual payment standard.
