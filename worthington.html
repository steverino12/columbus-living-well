Worthington: a renting and living guide

What landlords and tenants should know about Worthington—a separate city on the Olentangy River north of Columbus with its own income tax, zoning code, and design review—including why short-term rentals are off the table, how the Architectural Review Board shapes Old Worthington, COTA service on High Street and the changes proposed for 2027, Worthington Schools enrollment, the four charter questions on the November 2026 ballot, and voucher figures for ZIP 43085, without inventing market rents.

Last updated October 28, 2026. Facts below come from the City of Worthington and its codified ordinances, Worthington City Council records, Worthington Schools, the Ohio Department of Taxation, COTA, the Franklin County Board of Elections, Worthington Pulse, ThisWeek Community News, the U.S. Census Bureau (via Wikipedia’s summary of the 2020 count), and CMHA tables cited in Sources. Asking-rent medians are not invented here; see Rent Pulse and Fair Market Rents for verified figures.

A separate city, not a Columbus neighborhood

Worthington is its own municipality in Franklin County, a northern suburb on the Olentangy River. Except for a small border with Perry Township on its west side, it is completely surrounded by Columbus; together with tiny Riverlea, it forms an enclave inside Columbus. The city covers about 5.6 square miles and had 14,786 residents at the 2020 Census. Interstate 270, U.S. Route 23 (High Street), State Route 161, and State Route 315 pass through or alongside it, and City Hall—the Louis J.R. Goorey Worthington Municipal Building—is at 6550 N. High Street.

That independence matters for a rental. Columbus City Code does not apply inside Worthington’s city limits, so Columbus-only rules such as the source-of-income protection in Columbus City Code 4551.03 do not reach Worthington properties; Worthington’s own codified ordinances and Ohio’s statewide landlord-tenant law in Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5321 do. The catch is the mailing address. ZIP 43085 and the Worthington School District both reach well beyond the city, so a “Worthington” address can be inside the City of Columbus. The City’s “In Worthington?” map shows the city limits, the school district, and the ZIP area side by side, and the Franklin County Auditor confirms the taxing district for a parcel.

The 2020 Census counted 6,119 housing units in Worthington, 4.1% of them vacant, and 5,866 households. The homeowner vacancy rate was 0.7% and the rental vacancy rate was 5.5%. About 34.6% of households had children under 18, and the median age was 42.1.

How Worthington began

Worthington is one of central Ohio’s oldest planned towns. The Scioto Company formed in Granby, Connecticut, in May 1802, with James Kilbourne as president, to settle in the Ohio Country. It bought 16,000 acres along the Whetstone River—now the Olentangy—at $1.50 an acre, and Kilbourne arrived to inspect the land in May 1803. That August the company named the village for Thomas Worthington, one of Ohio’s first two U.S. senators and later its governor, and asked each member to contribute $2 toward a library. By December 1803 the village was laid out in 160 three-quarter-acre lots around a five-acre public green at the center of the village, on what is now High Street.

Kilbourne’s 1808 commercial building at 679–681 High Street is described as the oldest commercial building in continuous use in Ohio, and the Worthington Inn began as the Union Hotel in 1866. Growth was slow for more than a century—the 1950 Census counted 2,141 people—before postwar subdivisions pushed the population to 9,239 in 1960 and 15,326 in 1970. It has hovered between about 13,500 and 15,000 since. That history explains the city’s two faces: a historic village core around the Green, and mid-century neighborhoods that make up most of the housing stock.

Short-term rentals: not permitted

Worthington is one of the stricter places in central Ohio for short-term rentals. Its Planning and Zoning Code defines a “dwelling unit” as a housekeeping unit for owner occupancy “or rental or lease on a monthly or longer basis.” Ordinance 10-2020, passed May 18, 2020 and effective July 20, 2020, also added a definition of “transient guests” to the code’s definition of a tourist home: people renting, using, or occupying a dwelling for 30 consecutive calendar days or less. As ThisWeek Community News reported when council took up the change, the City’s director of planning and building told council that Worthington does not permit tourist homes, or short-term rentals.

In practice, that means a landlord should plan on leases of at least a month, and a tenant should not count on subletting a room or the whole unit through a nightly or weekly platform. Investors comparing Worthington with cities that allow owner-occupied hosting, such as Bexley or Grandview Heights, should treat that as a hard difference. If you have a specific arrangement in mind, ask Planning and Building at (614) 431-2424 before you list anything.

Design review in Old Worthington

Exterior changes to buildings and property in the Architectural Review District need approval from the Architectural Review Board (ARB). Under Section 1177.04 of the codified ordinances, the board is made up of the members of the Municipal Planning Commission plus two members appointed annually by Council to represent the district, at least one of whom must be a resident property owner there. The City describes it as a seven-member board whose decisions can be appealed to City Council.

Section 1177.05 lists what the board weighs before issuing a certificate of appropriateness: height, massing, window treatment, porches and other exterior details, roof shape, materials and color, landscape and lighting, the pedestrian environment, signage, and sustainable features such as solar panels and rain barrels. An application needs four or more votes to pass. Staff memos also apply the Worthington Design Guidelines; recent 2026 approvals for porch enclosures in the Worthington Historic District, for example, note that additions should sit to the rear, stay subordinate to the house, and use traditional, compatible materials.

The ARB and Planning Commission generally meet at 7:00 p.m. on the second and fourth Thursdays of the month at the Municipal Building, with exceptions listed on the City’s 2026 schedule; meetings are live-streamed and archived. For a landlord, the practical lesson is to price ARB time into any exterior project—new windows, a porch, a fence, or siding—on a property in the district. For a tenant, it means exterior changes are not something a landlord can promise on a short timeline.

Zoning is also in the news. In July 2026, Council rezoned the Boundless campus on East Dublin-Granville Road for an apartment project; a resident group then gathered enough valid signatures for a referendum, and Worthington Pulse reports that the rezoning goes to voters in November 2027 rather than this year. Anyone tracking new rental supply in Worthington should follow that vote.

Getting around: COTA on High Street

COTA’s Line 102 (Polaris Pkwy/N High) runs on High Street through Worthington between Downtown Columbus and Polaris Fashion Place, with stops serving the Crosswoods and Delawanda park-and-rides, The Ohio State University, and the Short North along the way. It makes all stops north of Morse Road and limited stops between Morse Road and Nationwide Boulevard. COTA classifies Line 102 as a standard line, meaning buses 16 to 60 minutes apart. Standard fares are $2, with free two-hour transfers and a $4.50 daily cap when you pay digitally.

Changes are coming. COTA’s 2027–2031 Short Range Transit Plan, presented to Worthington City Council in May 2026, proposes replacing Line 102 with an extension of the frequent Line 2 up High Street, keeping 30-minute service through Worthington, adding 30-minute weekend service north of the Westview turnaround in place of today’s hourly weekend service, and making Line 2 part of COTA’s overnight network. The plan also proposes extending Line 35 west along Wilson Bridge Road and discontinuing Line 74. Worthington Pulse reports that some commuters worry the loss of the limited-stop trip could lengthen their ride downtown. Check current timetables before you sign a lease that depends on the bus.

Schools and enrollment

Worthington Schools is a separate district from Columbus City Schools, headquartered at 200 E. Wilson Bridge Road. Its boundaries cover the City of Worthington and large parts of neighboring Columbus, Perry Township, and Sharon Township and reach north to the Delaware County line and Lazelle Road. The district publishes a street-by-street boundary list, and its enrollment page says families must live within the district to enroll.

For landlords this has a practical consequence. Enrollment requires a current lease with signature pages (or a mortgage statement) plus a utility bill dated within 30 days. Families who live with a relative or friend must file a notarized shared-residency affidavit, and the district’s residency form says it may investigate residency and asks families to allow release of rental and utility information. A complete, signed lease that names everyone living in the unit makes enrollment smoother.

Taxes for tenants who move here

Worthington levies a 2.5% municipal income tax under Section 1716.01 of its codified ordinances, collected by the Regional Income Tax Agency (RITA). Unlike some neighboring cities, Worthington gives residents a 100% credit, up to its 2.5% rate, for municipal income tax paid to the city where they work. The City’s resident tax guide gives the examples: a resident who works in Columbus pays Columbus’s 2.5% and owes Worthington nothing more, while a resident who works in Dublin, at 2.0%, generally owes Worthington the 0.5% difference. All residents 18 and older must file an annual Worthington return with RITA, even if they owe nothing, and residents who work from home in Worthington more than 20 days a year should make sure their employer accounts for those days.

Worthington City Schools does not appear on the Ohio Department of Taxation’s list of school districts levying an income tax effective January 1, 2026, so there is no separate school district income tax to budget for. Instead, the City notes that most of a Worthington property tax bill goes to Worthington Schools, and property taxes often reach tenants indirectly through rent.

On the November 3, 2026 ballot

Worthington voters will see four city charter amendments, Issues 35 through 38, according to the Franklin County Board of Elections’ certified list. Worthington Pulse summarizes them this way: Issue 35 would shorten the window for collecting signatures against a zoning change from 60 days to 30; Issue 36 would set referendum signatures at 10% of the city’s voters in the last governor’s race; Issue 37 bundles the Charter Review Commission’s other recommendations, mainly merging three development review boards into one; and Issue 38, which came from a resident petition, would set signatures at 10% of voters in the last city election and apply to any ordinance or other measure. The county’s list shows no Worthington Schools tax issue on this ballot. Because these questions affect how zoning decisions—including new apartments—can be challenged, renters and owners alike may want to follow the result. This guide takes no position on any issue.

What landlords and tenants should check before signing

  • Which city. Confirm the parcel is inside Worthington, not a Columbus address with a 43085 ZIP; taxes and codes differ.
  • Which school district. Check the address against the Worthington Schools boundary list, which does not match city limits.
  • Lease length. Plan on terms of a month or longer; short-term rentals of 30 days or less are not permitted.
  • Exterior changes. In the Architectural Review District, budget time for an ARB certificate of appropriateness.
  • Everyone on the lease. School enrollment needs a signed lease and a recent utility bill, so list every occupant.
  • Local taxes. Budget for the 2.5% city income tax and file the annual RITA return, even when the work-city credit covers it.
  • Bus access. If you don’t drive, check distance to High Street and watch for COTA’s 2027 route changes.

Frequently asked questions

Is Worthington part of Columbus?

No. Worthington is its own city in Franklin County, founded in 1803 by the Scioto Company led by James Kilbourne. Except for a small border with Perry Township on the west, it is surrounded by Columbus. It has its own council, income tax, zoning code, and building regulation, so Columbus City Code rules do not apply to properties inside city limits. Many Columbus addresses also use ZIP 43085, so check the City’s “In Worthington?” map for a specific address.

What income tax do Worthington residents pay?

Worthington levies a 2.5% municipal income tax, collected by the Regional Income Tax Agency (RITA). Residents get a 100% credit, up to 2.5%, for municipal tax paid where they work, so a resident who works in Columbus generally owes Worthington nothing more, while one who works in a 2.0% city such as Dublin generally owes the 0.5% difference. All residents 18 and older must file an annual return with RITA. Worthington City Schools does not appear on the Ohio Department of Taxation’s 2026 list of school districts with an income tax.

Can I run an Airbnb in Worthington?

Generally no. Worthington’s zoning code defines a dwelling unit as housing for owner occupancy or rental on a monthly or longer basis, and Ordinance 10-2020 defines a tourist home’s transient guests as people staying 30 consecutive days or less. When council passed that ordinance in 2020, the City’s planning director told council that Worthington does not permit tourist homes, or short-term rentals. Plan on leases of a month or longer, and ask Planning and Building before trying anything else.

Which COTA lines serve Worthington?

Line 102 (Polaris Pkwy/N High) runs on High Street through Worthington between Downtown Columbus and Polaris, making all stops north of Morse Road. COTA classifies it as a standard line, with buses 16 to 60 minutes apart. COTA’s 2027–2031 Short Range Transit Plan proposes replacing Line 102 with an extension of Line 2 and extending Line 35 west along Wilson Bridge Road, starting in 2027.

What is on the November 3, 2026 ballot in Worthington?

Four city charter amendments, Issues 35 through 38. Issue 35 would shorten the zoning referendum window from 60 days to 30, Issues 36 and 38 offer two different ways to lower the referendum signature requirement, and Issue 37 bundles the Charter Review Commission’s other changes, mainly merging three development review boards into one. The county’s certified issue list shows no Worthington Schools levy on this ballot.

What is the voucher payment standard in Worthington?

Worthington uses ZIP 43085. CMHA’s FY2027 Small Area Fair Market Rents, effective October 1, 2026, at 100% of SAFMR, list $1,450 for a studio, $1,550 for one bedroom, $1,850 for two bedrooms, $2,200 for three bedrooms, and $2,510 for four bedrooms. These are payment-standard benchmarks, not asking rents.
