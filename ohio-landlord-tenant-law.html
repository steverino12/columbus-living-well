Landlord-Tenant Law · Ohio & Columbus

Ohio landlord-tenant law basics for Columbus renters and landlords

A plain-language walk through the state rules in Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5321, the eviction notice rules in Chapter 1923, and the extra protections that apply inside Columbus city limits.

Last updated October 15, 2026. Every rule below links to the statute or ordinance in Sources. This is a general explainer, not legal advice; read the current text and talk to an attorney about a specific dispute.

Where the rules come from

Most residential rental relationships in Ohio are governed by Chapter 5321 of the Ohio Revised Code (ORC), titled Landlords and Tenants. It sets out what landlords must do, what tenants must do, how security deposits work, and what a tenant can do when repairs are not made. Evictions, which Ohio law calls forcible entry and detainer actions, follow Chapter 1923.

A lease can add terms, but only terms that are consistent with Chapter 5321 and other law (ORC 5321.06). Under ORC 5321.13, the protections in the chapter generally cannot be waived by a lease, and several kinds of clauses are not recognized at all: a confession-of-judgment clause, an agreement that the tenant will pay the landlord’s attorney fees, and a clause that limits the landlord’s legal liability or makes the tenant indemnify the landlord for it.

On top of state law, the City of Columbus has its own housing code and rental rules in the Columbus City Codes. Those local rules apply to properties within the City of Columbus. Suburbs such as Dublin, Westerville, Grove City, or Upper Arlington have their own codes, so check the city where the property actually sits.

What an Ohio landlord must do

ORC 5321.04 lists the core landlord duties. A landlord must:

  • Comply with building, housing, health, and safety codes that materially affect health and safety.
  • Make repairs and do whatever is reasonably necessary to keep the premises fit and habitable.
  • Keep common areas safe and sanitary.
  • Keep electrical, plumbing, sanitary, heating, ventilating, and air-conditioning fixtures and appliances (and elevators) that the landlord supplies in good and safe working order.
  • Provide trash receptacles and removal when the landlord has rental agreements covering four or more units in the same structure.
  • Supply running water, reasonable hot water, and reasonable heat at all times, with limited exceptions such as heat or hot water that the tenant controls through a direct utility connection.
  • Give reasonable notice before entering and enter only at reasonable times, except in an emergency.

Every written lease must also contain the name and address of the owner and of any agent (ORC 5321.18). If the landlord leaves that out, the tenant’s written-notice requirements for repair remedies are treated as waived by the landlord.

What an Ohio tenant must do

ORC 5321.05 puts matching duties on tenants. A tenant must keep their part of the premises safe and sanitary, dispose of trash properly, keep plumbing fixtures as clean as their condition permits, use electrical and plumbing fixtures properly, and follow the housing, health, and safety codes that apply to tenants. Tenants are responsible for not damaging the property themselves and for making sure their guests do not either, and for not disturbing neighbors’ peaceful enjoyment. If a written lease requires the tenant to maintain landlord-supplied appliances such as a refrigerator or washer, that duty applies too.

If a tenant fails a duty that materially affects health and safety, ORC 5321.11 lets the landlord give written notice describing the problem and a termination date at least 30 days after the tenant receives it. If the tenant fixes the problem, the lease continues; if not, it ends on the date in the notice.

Landlord entry and notice

Tenants may not unreasonably refuse entry for inspections, repairs, deliveries of large parcels, agreed services, or showing the unit to buyers, lenders, prospective tenants, or contractors (ORC 5321.05(B)). In return, the landlord must give reasonable notice and come at reasonable times. Twenty-four hours is presumed to be reasonable notice unless there is evidence to the contrary (ORC 5321.04(A)(8)).

If a landlord enters without proper notice, enters in an unreasonable manner, or makes repeated demands that amount to harassment, the tenant may recover actual damages, seek a court order to stop it, recover reasonable attorney fees, or end the lease (ORC 5321.04(B)).

Security deposits

Ohio does not set a statewide cap on how large a security deposit can be, but ORC 5321.16 controls how it is handled:

  • Return deadline. Within 30 days after the lease ends and the tenant gives up possession, the landlord must deliver a written, itemized list of any deductions along with the balance owed.
  • Allowed deductions. Past-due rent and damages caused by the tenant’s failure to meet ORC 5321.05 or the lease.
  • Forwarding address. The tenant must give the landlord a forwarding address in writing. Without it, the tenant loses the right to the extra damages and attorney fees described next.
  • Penalty. If the landlord does not comply, the tenant can recover the amount wrongfully withheld, plus damages equal to that amount, plus reasonable attorney fees.
  • Interest. When a deposit is larger than $50 or one month’s rent (whichever is greater) and the tenant stays six months or more, the excess earns 5% interest per year, paid annually.

Inside Columbus, the Franklin County Law Library’s summary of city code notes that Columbus City Code 4551.04 gives new tenants the choice to pay a security deposit upfront or in installments over three or six months. Landlords operating in the city should build that option into their move-in paperwork.

Repairs and rent escrow

Ohio does not let a tenant simply stop paying rent because repairs are overdue. Doing that can lead to an eviction for nonpayment. Instead, ORC 5321.07 sets out a specific process:

  1. The tenant gives the landlord written notice describing the problem, sent to the person or place where rent is normally paid.
  2. The landlord has a reasonable time to fix it, considering how serious it is, or 30 days, whichever is sooner.
  3. If the problem is not fixed and the tenant is current on rent, the tenant may deposit rent with the clerk of the municipal or county court, ask the court to order the repair (and possibly reduce rent or use deposited rent for the repair), or end the lease.

The rent-deposit remedy has two important exceptions. It does not apply to a landlord who has rental agreements covering three or fewer units and who discloses that in the written lease (or in a written notice at move-in for an oral tenancy). It also does not apply to student tenants. In Columbus, the clerk for these deposits is the Franklin County Municipal Court.

Ending a tenancy

A fixed-term lease generally ends on the date it states, under its own renewal terms. For periodic tenancies with no fixed end date, ORC 5321.17 sets minimum notice:

Tenancy typeMinimum notice to end or not renew
Week-to-weekAt least 7 days before the termination date in the notice
Month-to-monthAt least 30 days before the periodic rental date

The month-to-month rule is measured from the rent due date, not just “30 days from today.” A notice given on the 15th of a month for a tenancy with rent due on the 1st generally will not be effective until the end of the following rental period.

Evictions: notice, court, and no self-help

Before filing an eviction case, a landlord must give the tenant a written notice to leave at least three days before filing (ORC 1923.04). It can be delivered in person, left at the tenant’s home or the premises, or sent by certified mail with return receipt. For residential property, the notice must conspicuously include this statutory language: “You are being asked to leave the premises. If you do not leave, an eviction action may be initiated against you. If you are in doubt regarding your legal rights and obligations as a tenant, it is recommended that you seek legal assistance.”

Only a court can order a tenant out. ORC 5321.15 forbids landlords from shutting off utilities or services, locking a tenant out, or threatening unlawful acts to regain possession, and from seizing a tenant’s belongings to collect rent without a court order. A landlord who does so is liable for the tenant’s damages plus reasonable attorney fees. For Columbus properties, eviction cases are heard in the Franklin County Municipal Court.

Columbus “pay to stay” and late-fee limit

Columbus City Code 4551.07, as amended by Ordinance 2109-2023 (passed August 2, 2023), gives tenants in the city an affirmative defense to an eviction for nonpayment. If the tenant, or an agency or person paying on the tenant’s behalf, offers all past-due rent plus reasonable late fees before the case is filed, or offers that amount plus court costs before judgment, and the landlord refuses it, the tenant can raise that refusal as a defense. It does not stop evictions based on other lease violations.

The same section limits late fees where a lease allows them: the total late fee for any month may not exceed the larger of $50 or 10% of the monthly contract rent. Related city rules listed by the Franklin County Law Library require landlords to give receipts for rent payments (CC 4551.05) and bar refusing rent just because someone other than the tenant is paying it (CC 4551.06).

Retaliation is prohibited

Under ORC 5321.02, a landlord may not raise rent, cut services, or bring or threaten an eviction because a tenant complained to a government agency about a code violation, complained to the landlord about a violation of ORC 5321.04, or organized with other tenants. A tenant facing retaliation can use it as a defense, recover possession, or end the lease, and can recover actual damages and attorney fees. Landlords keep some rights: ORC 5321.02(C) allows rent increases that reflect improvements or higher operating costs, and ORC 5321.03 still allows an eviction when the tenant is behind on rent, caused the code violation, or is holding over after the term. Columbus City Code 4509.07, according to the Franklin County Law Library, makes retaliation against a tenant for reporting code violations a criminal misdemeanor.

Columbus source-of-income protection

Columbus City Code 4551.03, adopted through Ordinance 0494-2021, makes it a first-degree misdemeanor for a landlord in the city to knowingly refuse to rent, or to set different prices, terms, or fees, based on a tenant’s lawful source of income. The ordinance defines source of income broadly, including wages, Social Security, SSI, child and spousal support, public assistance, and rent vouchers. If a landlord uses a minimum-income rule, the voucher or subsidy amount must be subtracted from the rent before the income test is applied.

A quick checklist

Renters:

  • Make sure the lease lists the owner’s and any agent’s name and address.
  • Photograph the unit at move-in and move-out.
  • Put repair requests in writing and keep copies; stay current on rent if you may need rent escrow.
  • Give a written forwarding address when you move out.
  • If you receive a three-day notice, read it carefully and seek legal help early.

Landlords:

  • Include owner and agent contact information in every written lease.
  • Give at least 24 hours’ notice before non-emergency entry.
  • Return deposits with an itemized list within 30 days of move-out and possession.
  • In Columbus, keep late fees within the larger of $50 or 10% of rent, issue rent receipts, and accept voucher income on equal terms.
  • Use the court process for possession; never change locks or shut off utilities.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an Ohio landlord have to return a security deposit?

Under Ohio Revised Code 5321.16, the landlord must deliver the deposit balance, with any deductions itemized in a written notice, within 30 days after the rental agreement ends and the tenant gives up possession. The tenant must give the landlord a forwarding address in writing to keep the right to extra damages and attorney fees.

How much notice must a landlord give before entering in Ohio?

Ohio Revised Code 5321.04 requires reasonable notice and entry at reasonable times, except in an emergency or when notice is impracticable. Twenty-four hours is presumed to be reasonable notice unless there is evidence to the contrary.

Can I withhold rent in Ohio if my landlord will not make repairs?

Not by simply keeping it. Under Ohio Revised Code 5321.07, a tenant who is current on rent may give written notice of the problem and, if it is not fixed within a reasonable time or 30 days (whichever is sooner), deposit rent with the clerk of the municipal court, ask the court to order repairs, or end the lease. The escrow remedy does not apply to student tenants or to landlords with three or fewer units who gave written notice of that fact.

What notice is required before an eviction in Ohio?

Before filing an eviction (forcible entry and detainer) case, Ohio Revised Code 1923.04 requires a written notice to leave delivered at least three days in advance, containing specific statutory language about seeking legal assistance. Only a court can order a tenant removed; lockouts and utility shutoffs to force a tenant out are prohibited by Ohio Revised Code 5321.15.

Is there a cap on late fees in Columbus?

Inside the City of Columbus, Columbus City Code 4551.07 (amended by Ordinance 2109-2023) limits a monthly late fee to the larger of $50 or 10% of the monthly contract rent. Ohio state law has no equivalent statewide cap.

Can a Columbus landlord refuse a Housing Choice Voucher?

Columbus City Code 4551.03 prohibits landlords within city limits from refusing to rent based on a lawful source of income, which includes rent vouchers and other assistance. If a landlord uses an income threshold, the voucher or subsidy amount must be subtracted from the rent before applying it.
